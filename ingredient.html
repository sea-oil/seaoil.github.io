A NATURAL WORLD

The sea has its own gardens.

Posidonia oceanica, also known as Neptune grass, is not a seaweed. It is a true flowering plant adapted to life underwater. Its meadows help define Mediterranean coastal ecosystems.

THE HISTORY OF NEPTUNE GRASS

A botanical world beneath the Mediterranean.

Posidonia oceanica, commonly known as Neptune grass or Mediterranean tapeweed, is a seagrass species endemic to the Mediterranean Sea. It forms large underwater meadows that are an important part of the ecosystem. Unlike seaweed, it is a true flowering plant with roots, rhizomes, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds.

The name Posidonia refers to Poseidon, the Greek god of the sea. Carl Linnaeus described the species under the genus Zostera; the name Posidonia oceanica was published by Alire Raffeneau Delile in 1813, in Description de l’Égypte. Its botanical story has been studied and documented for centuries.

How the underwater meadows grow

Posidonia oceanica has roots, which mainly anchor the plant to the seabed, underground rhizomes, and long, ribbon-like leaves. Horizontal rhizomes allow the meadow to spread across the seafloor, while vertical rhizomes respond to accumulating sediment. Over time the roots, rhizomes and trapped sediments form substantial underwater structures.

Its leaves are bright green when young and turn brown as they age. They can reach approximately 1.5 metres in length and are often arranged in groups of six or seven. Old outer leaves are shed in autumn, while fresh growth appears during winter. The slow growth of its rhizomes helps explain why established meadows are ecologically valuable and slow to recover when damaged.

Flowers, fruit and the “olive of the sea”

Although it grows underwater, this plant reproduces using flowers and fruit. The flowers are hermaphroditic, pollination occurs through water, and the fruit matures before detaching and floating toward the surface. In Italy the floating fruits are sometimes called l’oliva di mare — “the olive of the sea.” Their buoyancy helps disperse seeds to new areas of seabed.

It also spreads asexually through creeping rhizomes. These gradually extend existing meadows, creating habitat used by many Mediterranean marine organisms.

What washes ashore

Detached leaves and fibres sometimes collect along Mediterranean coastlines. Fibrous balls formed from this material are known as egagropili or Neptune balls. These objects offer another glimpse of the plant’s life cycle outside the underwater meadow.

The leaves and their life cycle

The plant’s tape-shaped leaves emerge from upright rhizomes. They typically have parallel ribs and rounded tips, and the outer ends may be worn away by waves and currents. The leaves form bundles in which older leaves grow on the outside and younger leaves remain nearer the center.

Botanists distinguish adult leaves, intermediate leaves and young leaves. Adult leaves have a photosynthetic blade and a distinct basal region; intermediate leaves lack that distinct base; very young leaves are often shorter than 50 millimetres. The oldest outer leaves turn brown, lose photosynthetic activity and are shed seasonally.

Sexual reproduction under water

Posidonia oceanica reproduces sexually by forming flowers and fruits. Its flowers are grouped in a herringbone-shaped inflorescence and are enclosed by floral bracts. They contain both female and male reproductive structures. Flowering typically takes place in autumn in shallower meadows, with later flowering in some deeper areas.

Pollen is released into the water, where it can reach another flower. After fertilization, the fruit develops over several months. Mature fruit detaches from the plant and floats. As the fruit breaks down, the seed falls toward the seabed and may germinate when the depth, sediment and water movement are suitable. Research has examined how exposure to waves and different seabed materials can affect young seedlings.

Slow growth, long-lasting meadows

The plant also reproduces asexually through horizontal rhizomes, gradually extending an existing meadow. This spreading is slow compared with many other marine plants. Horizontal rhizomes may grow only a few centimetres per year, and vertical rhizomes also grow gradually. Leaves can require weeks to develop. These slow processes help explain why a mature meadow can take a long time to rebuild after disturbance.

How scientists classify the plant

Its scientific classification places Posidonia oceanica in the plant kingdom (Plantae), within the flowering plants (Angiosperms) and monocots. It belongs to the order Alismatales, the family Posidoniaceae, the genus Posidonia, and the species P. oceanica. Botanical classification has changed as scientists have revised relationships among aquatic flowering plants.

A living Mediterranean heritage

Posidonia oceanica meadows support marine biodiversity, provide nursery areas for organisms, and help stabilize sediments. Their environmental role is part of why protecting these habitats matters. The founder has identified this seagrass as an ingredient in C-OIL’s formulation.

Its history connects the underwater landscape, an unusual fruit, centuries of botanical study and modern interest in plant extracts. This botanical history is distinct from the history, preparation and testing of the finished C-OIL formula.

BOTANICAL RESEARCH

What researchers have explored.

Scientists have studied extracts from beach-cast Posidonia oceanica leaves for phytochemical composition and antioxidant activity. One published study investigated an extract incorporated into an experimental topical cream and evaluated effects in laboratory assays and cultured human skin cells.

These studies concern experimental preparations, not the final C-OIL product. The specific extract preparation, purity, concentration, stability and finished-product testing matter when interpreting results.
